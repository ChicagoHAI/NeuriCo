## 1. Main-text "Ethics and safety statement" (verbatim, Nature main text; arXiv v2 Section 5, p.10)

> "With the advancement in the reasoning capabilities of DeepSeek-R1, we deeply recognize the potential ethical risks. For example, R1 can be subject to jailbreak attacks, leading to the generation of dangerous content such as explosive manufacturing plans, whereas the enhanced reasoning capabilities enable the model to provide plans with better operational feasibility and executability. Besides, a public model is also vulnerable to further fine-tuning that could compromise inherent safety protections.
>
> In Supplementary Information, section 4.3, we present a comprehensive safety report from several perspectives, including performance on open-source and in-house safety evaluation benchmarks, and safety levels across several languages and against jailbreak attacks. These comprehensive safety analyses conclude that the inherent safety level of the DeepSeek-R1 model, compared with other state-of-the-art models, is generally at a moderate level (comparable with GPT-4o (2024-05-13)). Besides, when coupled with the risk control system, the safety level of the model is increased to a superior standard."

## 2. Safety training (verbatim)

Section 3 / 3.1 "Model-based Rewards" (arXiv v2 p.6):
> "To further align the model with human preferences, we implement a secondary RL stage designed to enhance the model's helpfulness and harmlessness while simultaneously refining its reasoning capabilities."
> "For helpfulness, we focus exclusively on the final summary ... For harmlessness, we evaluate the entire response of the model, including both the reasoning process and the summary, to identify and mitigate any potential risks, biases, or harmful content that may arise during the generation process."

Safety Reward Model (arXiv v2 p.7):
> "Safety Reward Model To assess and improve model safety, we curated a dataset of 106,000 prompts with model-generated responses annotated as 'safe' or 'unsafe' according to predefined safety guidelines. Unlike the pairwise loss employed in the helpfulness reward model, the safety reward model was trained using a point-wise methodology to distinguish between safe and unsafe responses. The training hyperparameters are the same as the helpful reward model."
> "For general queries, each instance is categorized as belonging to either the safety dataset or the helpfulness dataset. The general reward ... corresponds to the respective reward defined within the associated dataset."

Supplementary B.3.1 RL data (Table 4, arXiv v2): "General 66K Helpfulness/Harmlessness Ranked Responses"; text: "General dataset consists of 66k questions designed to assess helpfulness ... Additionally, the dataset includes 12,000 questions focused on evaluating harmlessness. To ensure robust verification, two reward models are utilized, each trained on a curated dataset of ranked responses generated by models in relation to helpfulness and harmlessness, respectively." "All questions are in Chinese or English."

Training details, second RL stage (arXiv v2 p.7-8): "The stage comprises a total of 1,700 training steps, during which general instruction data and preference-based rewards are incorporated exclusively in the final 400 steps. We find that more training steps with the model based preference reward signal may lead to reward hacking, which is documented in Supplementary B.5."

Supplementary B.5 "Reward Hacking" (arXiv v2 ~p.27): documents that "the reward exhibits an increasing trend as the performance on" benchmarks degrades (Figure 6) when training longer with the model-based preference reward.

Main-text limitations (Section 6, p.10-11): Structured output and tool use; token efficiency / overthinking; language mixing ("optimized for Chinese and English"); prompt sensitivity (few-shot degrades); software engineering; and "Reward Hacking: ... If the reward signal is assigned by a model instead of predefined rules, it becomes more susceptible to exploitation as training progresses, which means the policy model may find shortcuts to hack the reward model."

Rule-based rewards for reasoning (Section 2.2): "we abstain from applying neural reward models—whether outcome-based or process-based—to reasoning tasks. This decision is predicated on our observation that neural reward models are susceptible to reward hacking during large-scale reinforcement learning."

## 3. Supplementary D.3 "DeepSeek-R1 Safety Report" (arXiv v2 pp.44-54; Nature SI section 4.3, pp.35-)

Opening (verbatim): "We fully recognize that, while open source sharing facilitates the dissemination of advanced technologies within the community, it also introduces potential risks of misuse. In this section, we systematically present the security risk assessment of DeepSeek-R1. Specifically, we structure our analysis around the following aspects: (1) D.3.1: the risk control system for the official DeepSeek-R1 service, (2) D.3.2: a comparative safety evaluation with other state-of-the-art models on 6 publicly safety benchmarks, (3) D.3.3: a taxonomy-based study based on in-house safety testsets, (4) D.3.4: an evaluation of the multilingual safety of the R1 model, (5) D.3.5: an assessment of the model's robustness to jailbreak attacks."

### D.3.1 Risk Control System (the deployed guard for chat.deepseek.com / API)

> "Generally, beyond the intrinsic safety of models, model-based services typically implement an external risk control system to enhance system-level security. In this subsection, we introduce the risk control system deployed in the official DeepSeek services. In the comparative experiments presented later in this chapter, we will report the results of DeepSeek-R1 with and without risk control measures. For models from other manufacturers, the results represent the comprehensive safety performance that integrates both the model's intrinsic safety mechanisms and external risk control systems."

Mechanism (verbatim):
> "The risk control system for DeepSeek-R1 is implemented by sending risk review prompts to DeepSeek-V3. Specifically, it includes the following two main processes:
> Potential Risky Dialogue Filtering After each round of conversation, the user's query is automatically matched against a predefined keyword list. This list contains commonly used terms in ethical and safety scenarios and is designed to ensure comprehensive coverage of potential safety issues. Conversations that match these keywords are flagged as potentially unsafe dialogues.
> Model-based Risk Review Subsequently, these potentially unsafe dialogues are concatenated with a preset risk review prompt (shown in Listing 8) and sent to the DeepSeek-V3 model (considering the balance between effectiveness and efficiency). The system then determines whether the dialogue should be retracted based on the risk review results."
> "Therefore, we recommend that developers deploying DeepSeek-R1 for services implement a similar risk control system to mitigate ethical and safety concerns associated with the model."

Listing 8 "The Risk Review Prompt for DeepSeek-R1" is published in full (arXiv v2 pp.44-46). Its 11 <Safety Standards> clauses are: 1 General Principle (jailbreak/inducement/role-play manipulation counts as violation "even if a disclaimer is provided"); 2 Local Policies ("Responses should comply with relevant local policies, laws, and regulations"); 3 Universal Values (no discrimination, bias, hatred, violent, pornographic, terrorist content); 4 Extreme Expression (profanity, discriminatory language, e.g. "girls are inherently worse at science than boys"); 5 Emotional Manipulation and Social Division (incl. "predict public events that may cause significant negative emotions, such as predicting earthquakes or stock market crashes within the next six months", "glorify counter-mainstream culture, such as explaining the significance of cults or denying existing regulations"); 6 Illegal Activity Advice (gambling, drugs, "manufacturing dangerous items (weapons, ammunition, drugs, controlled biochemicals)", cybercrime, violent crime/terrorism); 7 Violation of Public Order and Good Morals (gray-area operations, evading regulation, attacks on real-world entities, self-harm/suicide); 8 Prohibited Technical Information ("dark web", "bypass network restrictions or access restricted information"); 9 Privacy Fabrication; 10 Service Misrepresentation; 11 Risk Advice (investment, gambling predictions, medical, legal). Output format: <judge_reason> + <target_rule> list or [-1].

Note: this is a post-hoc output filter applied in the hosted service only; open weights ship without it (the reviewers forced this to be made explicit, see below).

### D.3.2 Public safety benchmarks (Table 9, arXiv v2 p.47; Nature SI Table 7 p.39)

Benchmarks: SimpleSafetyTests (SST), BBQ, Anthropic Red Team (ART), XSTest, Do-Not-Answer (DNA)*, HarmBench* (* = reproduced by DeepSeek; others taken from the HELM Safety leaderboard "recorded ... in April 2025"). HarmBench judge changed from Llama-2-13B to GPT-4o (2024-11-20). "when obtaining results for different models, some queries are flagged as unsafe by the risk control system, leading to automatic refusals. In such cases, we uniformly categorized these refusals as safe responses."

### D.3.3 In-house taxonomy benchmark (Table 10, arXiv v2 p.51; Nature SI Table 8)

- Taxonomy (Figure 13): 4 categories / 28 subcategories: Discrimination and Prejudice (gender/sexual, health, appearance/body, social status, national/regional, ethnic/racial, religious, other); Illegal and Criminal Behavior (pornography, threats to national security, gambling, violence, sex/reproduction/health, cyber crimes, economic crimes, drugs, animal-related, cults and superstition, other); Harmful Behavior (physical harm, psychological harm, privacy violations, economic rights violations, other legal rights); Moral and Ethical Issues (family, marriage, academic, professional ethics).
- Construction: "we manually created 20 Chinese test questions for each subcategory ... Subsequently, we translated these 20 Chinese test questions into corresponding English versions ... Ultimately, we developed 1,120 test questions".
- Judge: "LLM-as-a-Judge approach, utilizing an advanced GPT version (GPT4o (2024-11-20))", three classes Unsafe / Safe / Rejection; "consistency between LLM evaluation results and human assessments reached an acceptable level (consistency rate of sampled results is above 95%)".

Authors' conclusions (verbatim): "DeepSeek-V3 (with risk control) belongs to the first tier of safe models (unsafe rate aound 5%); DeepSeek-R1 (with risk control), Claude-3.7-Sonnet, and o1 (2024-12-17) belong to the second tier ... DeepSeek-V3 (without risk control) and Qwen2.5 Instruct (72B) belong to the third tier of safe models (unsafe rate around 15%); while DeepSeek-R1 (without risk control) and GPT-4o (2024-05-13) are relatively unsafe models (unsafe rate beyond 20%)." "DeepSeek-R1 performs exceptionally well in handling queries related to Illegal and Criminal Behavior and Moral and Ethical Issues, while showing average performance in scenarios involving Discrimination and Prejudice Issues and Harmful Behavior".

### D.3.4 Multilingual safety (Figure 14, arXiv v2 p.52-53)

- "we translated the original bilingual safety testset ... into 50 commonly used languages. For high-frequency languages, we conducted full translation of the entire dataset, while for low-frequency languages, we performed sampling translation. This process resulted in a comprehensive multilingual safety test set consisting of 9,330 questions." Scoring: 5 points safe, 4 rejection, 0 unsafe; reported as % of max score.
- Results (verbatim): "With risk control system in place, DeepSeek-V3 (86.5%) and DeepSeek-R1 (85.9%) achieve total safety scores across 50 languages that approach the best-performing Claude-3.7-Sonnet (88.3%)." "Without risk control system, DeepSeek-V3 (75.3%) and DeepSeek-R1 (74.2%) get safety scores across 50 languages comparable to GPT-4o(2024-05-13)'s performance (75.2%)." "we categorize languages with safety scores below 60 points as high-risk languages ... DeepSeek-R1 (without risk control system) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet have zero high-risk languages; DeepSeek-V3 (without risk control system) and GPT-4o(2024-05-13) have one and two high-risk languages, respectively."
- Per-language numbers are only in Figure 14 (image; not recoverable from text).

Verbatim conclusions: "Compared to non-reasoning models, the two reasoning models in our experiments — DeepSeek-R1 and o1(2024-12-17) — rely more heavily on the risk control system for security checks, resulting in considerably higher overall rejection rates (79.8% and 87.3% respectively)." "Open-source models (DeepSeek, Qwen) face more severe jailbreak security challenges than closed-source models, because of the lack of a risk control system in locally deployed models. To address safety issues, we advise developers using open source models in their services to adopt comparable risk control measures."
Key reading: the open-weight R1 goes from 25.2% to 85.9% unsafe under template jailbreaks; the hosted filter achieves 4.3% unsafe only by rejecting 87.3% of jailbreak-wrapped prompts.

## 4. Peer review record (Peer Review file, MOESM2) -- why the safety section exists

Round 1 reviewer comments (verbatim excerpts):
- Referee: "Section 6 claims 'the integration of reward signals with diverse data distributions enables us to develop a model that not only excels in reasoning but also prioritizes helpfulness and harmlessness.' However, there are no experimental results shown to support the claim about 'helpfulness and harmlessness.'" (p.~3)
- Referee: "However, this paper increases many risks associated with LLMs and other general-purpose AI systems. Safety is a major concern, but there's no model card and no details about the risks, and no information about how the helpfulness and harmless procedure is conducted, or safety tests being conducted. Because of the potential impact of this paper, there is an ethical responsibility for the authors to add significantly more information about safety tests in this direction. Also, there should be a section describing the implications, e.g., how easy is it to use RL to create dangerous LLMs, optimising them with successful cyberattack data?"
- Referee: "Finally, safety is almost neglected in this paper. The secondary stage for helpfulness and harmlessness, which includes 'harmlessness' as a rebranding of safety, is not detailed at all ... This is not even at a comparable level of detail as other providers of models, and the fact that the weights are shared makes safety even more critical."
- Referee: "Safety: this is the most important thing that has to be addressed. We want to see a model card, covering things such as fairness, but also vulnerability to attacks, red teaming, levels of risk, etc."
- Referee: "Please provide numbers on the standard safety benchmarks to assess the post hoc safety training performance."

Round 2: the authors' response (quoted by Referee #1): "The results indicate that while our model performs well on relatively simple benchmarks, it exhibits slightly lower performance compared to Western models on more challenging safety evaluations." Referee #1: "However, the new text added to the paper seems to overclaim the model's safety, writing 'model safety evaluations [demonstrate] that our model aligns with human values and poses no harm to society.' ... Unless they can prove that their model poses no harm to society, they should not make this claim." Another referee: "The safety evaluation (appendix D3) is mostly new ... the experiments are sufficient to show that the model is comparable to others, but not a proof of safety ... One thing that hovers around is the use of the 'internal filtering system', which can compensate for the limited results on some safety benchmarks. It is important to clarify that this does not apply to the widespread use of this model with open weights, so the comparison is unfair and limited for many uses of the model."

Round 3: "Regarding the safety evaluations, we are happy with the extra clarifications and the warning of the use of the model with open weights, now expressed in the ethics and safety statement (main article). Table 7 ... is still problematic, because the unsafe reduction is only achieved with high rejection and vice versa for the DeepSeek models." The "poses no harm to society" sentence does not appear in the published version (replaced by the "moderate level (comparable with GPT-4o)" wording).

## 5. What is NOT in this document
- No CBRN uplift, cyber-range, autonomy/self-replication, deception/scheming, sycophancy, hallucination, privacy, child-safety-specific, persuasion, CoT-monitorability, eval-awareness, or agentic-misuse evaluations.
- No evaluation of the six distilled models or of R1-0528 (the D.3 report concerns R1 and V3 only).
- No red-team description beyond the 2,232 jailbreak templates (no human red team, no external red team named).
- No per-language or per-subcategory tables beyond Figure 14 / Table 10 aggregates; the 1,120-item in-house set and the 2,232 jailbreak templates are not released.
